Executive verdict
The literature suggests that elements of the proposal have been explored , but not the exact controller
design . Prior work has used solar/PV forecasts and EVΓÇÉcharging optimization (often in stations or
microgrids) and has begun to address forecast errors, but no paper appears to implement a home EV
charging controller that explicitly gates its decisions on forecast reliability . For example, Zhao and
Burke (2014) introduced solar PV forecasting into EV station energy managementπÇÉ81ΓÇáL52-L60πÇæ, and
Simolin et al. (2020) studied EV charging control under uncertainty in trip predictionsπÇÉ98ΓÇáL139-L146πÇæ.
Dong et al. (2025) proposed a stochastic scheduling framework for an EV charging station with PV
uncertaintyπÇÉ59ΓÇáL29-L37πÇæ. These and related works focus on incorporating uncertainty  into optimization
(often via robust or stochastic methods), but they do not implement a simple confidence-threshold
ΓÇ£fallbackΓÇ¥ strategy that the controller switches to when forecasts are deemed unreliable. In short, the
general idea of using forecasts for EV charging is well-studied, but a ΓÇ£confidence-awareΓÇ¥ controller that
conditionally switches strategies based on forecast error  appears to be a novel twist . The proposal seems 
incrementally novel : it combines known components (PV forecasting, EV scheduling, fallback rules) in a
way not explicitly covered by existing studies. Thus, it may be defensible as a small new contribution, but it
is close to the current state of the art (see TableΓÇ»C below for evidence).

Novelty analysis
Has confidence-aware charging been done?  Not explicitly. Existing works use forecasts or robust
optimization, but none switch strategies based on a forecast confidence threshold . Simolin et al.
did use a fallback (battery SOC) when EV travel predictions were unreliableπÇÉ98ΓÇáL139-L146πÇæ, which
is analogous, but applied to a different prediction (driver schedule, not PV output). Our proposed
idea extends this concept to PV forecast reliability. No paper was found that explicitly monitors solar
forecast error and changes charging strategy accordingly.
Have charging decisions been adjusted by PV forecast reliability?  We found no example where a
controller calculates forecast confidence (e.g. prediction interval width) and alters charging. Some
works (e.g. Shang 2026) analyze charging with PV under weather variabilityπÇÉ84ΓÇáL105-L113πÇæ, but
they do not operate  in real-time on reliability. They simply test different scenarios. In contrast, our
proposal would actively  slow or shift charging when forecasts are deemed unreliable. This specific
behavior appears to be unaddressed in the literature.
Have fallback schedules been used when forecasts are unreliable?  SimolinΓÇÖs battery-based
method is essentially a fallback when predictions failπÇÉ98ΓÇáL139-L146πÇæ, and robust optimization
frameworks (Dong et al.) implicitly guard against error , but we saw no simple fallback rule in home
EV charging with PV . Most optimization methods continuously incorporate uncertainty rather than
suddenly switching strategy.1.

Has the exact combination been evaluated?  The combination of (residential) smart-home EV
charging with PV forecasts and an explicit reliability-aware fallback  has not been explicitly
studied. The closest is Zhao 2014, which simply uses PV forecasts without accounting for errors
πÇÉ81ΓÇáL52-L60πÇæ, and Dong 2025, which does sophisticated stochastic planning but with a single fixed
optimization (no ΓÇ£if unreliable, do X else do YΓÇ¥)πÇÉ59ΓÇáL29-L37πÇæ. Thus the proposed controllerΓÇÖs
architecture seems new.
Are the chosen metrics already standard?  Yes. Metrics like electricity cost, peak demand, solar
utilization, deadlines, and penalty costs from forecast errors are commonly used in EV scheduling
and demand response studies. For example, Zhao reported peak/grid impactsπÇÉ81ΓÇáL52-L60πÇæ and
Dong reported cost/carbon changesπÇÉ59ΓÇáL29-L37πÇæ. None of these papers had a ΓÇ£confidence-awareΓÇ¥
variant to compare against, so our specific evaluation (especially quantifying extra cost from forecast
error) would be a new angle.
Unresolved issues:  The literature shows that forecast errors do impact scheduling outcomes  and that
robust or stochastic methods can mitigate this (e.g. Dong 2025πÇÉ59ΓÇáL29-L37πÇæ). What remains
unclear is how much a simple threshold-based strategy helps compared to these more complex methods .
Also, existing work largely targets public or workplace charging stations; residential smart-home
charging with price signals  is less explored. The interaction of forecast confidence  with user deadlines
and price  is not addressed anywhere as a distinct mechanism. These gaps suggest there is room to
test whether a confidence-aware policy can improve on naive scheduling.
Novelty assessment:  On balance, the proposal is incrementally novel . It blends established
components (PV forecasting, EV scheduling, fallback control) but in a new combination. We cannot
claim a completely new problem , since EV-PV integration has been studied, but the specific
algorithmic twist ΓÇô gating on forecast reliability ΓÇô appears to be novel . The closest precedent is
SimolinΓÇÖs fallback on EV need forecastsπÇÉ98ΓÇáL139-L146πÇæ, but our context (PV forecast errors, home
charging) is different. Thus the research might yield a publishable result, but it is best seen as an
incremental advance  over robust scheduling approaches.

Precise research question
Primary question:  Can an EVΓÇÉcharging controller that monitors solar PV forecast confidence and switches
to a conservative charging schedule when forecasts are unreliable significantly reduce costs or deadline
misses compared to non-adaptive methods? 
Secondary questions:  (1) How to quantify forecast reliability in real time (e.g. via prediction intervals or
historical error)? (2) How to choose the confidence threshold without peeking at test data? (3) What is the
trade-off between exploiting high solar use under good forecasts versus avoiding risk under bad forecasts? 4.

Proposed contribution
The implementation will be a home energy management algorithm  that explicitly uses a forecast
confidence measure  to adapt its EV charging schedule. The novel contribution is the decision rule: ΓÇ£if solar
forecast confidence < threshold, use fallback schedule; else use aggressive forecast-driven schedule.ΓÇ¥ Prior
methods either ignored PV uncertainty or used robust optimization, but none offered a simple,
implementable ΓÇ£confidence-gatingΓÇ¥ rule. By doing so, our work fills a gap: it shows whether a lightweight
reliability check can bridge the performance gap between naive forecast scheduling and full stochastic
optimization, in a realistic residential setting. All components (forecasting, scheduling, fallback) are simple,
making the approach easily reproducible in Python, yet the combination  targets a gap identified in related
work.
Methodology
Forecasting model:  Choose a solar PV forecast model (e.g. persistence, linear regression on cloud
cover , or a simple ML model using open data). Along with forecasts, compute a confidence estimate
(e.g. a standard deviation or quantile interval, possibly via quantile regression or conformal
prediction).
Reliability metric:  Define a reliability score (e.g. inverse of predicted variance or the width of a 95%
prediction interval). Determine a threshold for ΓÇ£sufficiently reliableΓÇ¥ based on historical cross-
validation (without peeking at the test set).
Charging controller:  Implement two scheduling modes:
a. Forecast-driven mode:  Optimize charging to maximize solar use / minimize cost  subject to meeting
the deadline. For example, this could be a linear program: charge more when price is low and solar is
predicted high.
b. Fallback mode:  A conservative schedule (e.g. evenly distribute charging over cheapest hours
regardless of solar , or simply charge at the end to guarantee meeting deadline with minimal grid
usage). 
The controller monitors forecast reliability each day (or each scheduling interval); if reliability falls below
threshold, it selects the fallback schedule, otherwise it uses the forecast-driven schedule.
Implementation:  Build a simulation in Python. At each simulation day: generate (or read) solar
forecasts and reliability; solve the chosen schedule; accumulate metrics. 
Baseline comparison:  Compare with (1) no-forecast (fixed or cheapest-only charging), (2) naive
forecast-driven (ignore reliability), and (3) an ideal ΓÇ£perfect forecastΓÇ¥ benchmark. Evaluate on
multiple days and scenarios.
All algorithms will be straightforward (no complex learning beyond simple forecasting), suitable for an
undergraduate project. 1.

Experimental plan
Data:  Use a public solar PV dataset (e.g. NRELΓÇÖs PV generation data or University of California Solar
dataset) and a household load profile. EV session arrival/departure can be synthesized (e.g. fixed
departure time, or random deadlines). Electricity prices can be a simple time-of-use tariff.
Forecast horizon:  For example, day-ahead hourly PV forecasts for the next 24ΓÇ»h. Schedule decisions
also on an hourly grid.
Forecast reliability:  Compute, say, the width of the central 90% prediction interval from the
forecasting model (using a quantile regression or empirical error). Or use the standard deviation
from an ensemble/persistence error model. The threshold can be chosen by a validation set (e.g. ΓÇ£if
predicted error > X kWΓÇ¥).
Confidence threshold selection:  Use cross-validation on historical data: try several thresholds (e.g.
percentiles of past error) and choose one that balances cost vs. risk on validation days, without using
test-day outcomes.
Fallback rule:  For example, if unreliable, schedule EV to charge after  solar peak in low-price hours
evenly (so risk of missing deadline is minimized). A simple rule: postpone charging to off-peak
overnight or late evening regardless of solar . 
EV parameters:  Choose a battery capacity (e.g. 40ΓÇ»kWh), a charging power rate (e.g. 7ΓÇ»kW), an energy
requirement (e.g. 20ΓÇ»kWh needed before morning), and a fixed departure time.
Metrics:  Total electricity cost, percent solar utilization (energy drawn vs. solar available), peak
household load, and deadline violation frequency/cost. Also measure extra cost incurred on days
with large forecast errors.
Baseline methods:
Fixed charging:  e.g. start charging at 80% SOC at 2ΓÇ»AM (common heuristic). 
Forecast-based (no reliability check):  optimize charging using the point forecast as if accurate. 
Proposed:  forecast-based with reliability check and fallback. 
Scenarios:  Create high-error vs. low-error days by perturbing forecasts (e.g. randomly degrade
prediction accuracy) to test robustness.
Experiments:  Run simulations over a year of data (with repeated random draws for uncertainty) and
report averages and confidence intervals. Include ablation: e.g. what if the threshold is set too low or
too high?

Scope:  The project is appropriately sized for an undergrad thesis: implementing a forecast model, a
